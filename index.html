What this is. A monitor of armed conflict and the machinery around it: offensives and incursions, strikes and bombardment, attacks on civilians, the arms industry and its contracts, budgets and military aid, bases and deployments, officials and command, law and accountability, nuclear and strategic forces, humanitarian consequence, and cyber and information operations.

It does not take a side, and it does not summarise. Headlines and snippets are the publishers' own, truncated but never reworded; no claim is assessed, no party is described, and no model touches the text. Every row links to the original. On a subject where the same event is reported irreconcilably from opposite directions, the honest thing a feed can do is show who published what and let you read them against each other — which is what the source labels are for.

Direction. Escalation — offensives launched, strikes carried out, weapons delivered, budgets raised, forces deployed, talks abandoned. De-escalation — ceasefires, truces, withdrawals, prisoner exchanges, aid corridors opened, treaties signed, sanctions lifted. Feeds of this subject overwhelmingly show the first; the second is marked so it can be found. A story can carry both, and often does: a ceasefire announced after a night of strikes.

Who reports it. Bodies, courts & ministries is UN mechanisms, the ICRC, NATO, courts and defence ministries — parties as well as arbiters, and labelled so you know which. Research & monitors is SIPRI, ACLED, Human Rights Watch, Amnesty, Bellingcat, Airwars. Defence trade press covers the industry from inside it. Press is general news across 25 languages, including outlets on several sides of ongoing wars.

Evidence. Each story is scored on what it carries: a documented act — confirmed, announced, signed, carried out, a verdict, a warrant (2); institutional material — a UN body, a court, a ministry, SIPRI, ACLED, satellite imagery, a commission of inquiry (2); a measured figure (1); a scheduled or pending step (1); a named place (1); and a primary source (1). At three or more the row is marked well documented. A score is not a judgement of truth — an official statement scores well and can still be false. It records what kind of thing the story is.

The map. Satellite imagery from Esri's free World Imagery layer — no account, no key, nothing tracked beyond the tiles your browser requests. Every story that resolved to a place gets its own pin, and clicking one opens that story at its publisher exactly as a row does. Stories resolving to the same country would sit on top of each other, so each is nudged onto a ring around the centroid: a country with forty stories shows forty pins fanned around it rather than one. The placement is deterministic, so a pin does not jump between renders. The pins are built from the same filtered set as the list, so every toggle above applies to both. Stories that named nowhere in particular are counted as unplaced under the map rather than guessed at and dropped somewhere false.

What a pin means. It is a point near the centroid of the most specific place the story resolved to — a country, or a subregion when only that was named — offset so it does not hide its neighbours. It is not the location of the event, and the offset is not a location either. A story about a strike in eastern Ukraine sits at the middle of Ukraine, because the feed reads headlines, not coordinates. Treat the map as a locator for what is being covered where, not as a map of events.

Region. Placement runs three levels deep: pick a region, then a subregion, then a place. Each row counts what it holds, and a story files under everything above whatever place it names.

What is refused. The metaphors, which are relentless in this vocabulary: price wars, bidding wars, culture wars, wars of words, battles for market share, battleground states. Sport, where attacks and battles are constant. The shooter franchises. The status line reports how many stories each harvest refused.

Why some stories are not mapped. A story is placed only if its headline or snippet names somewhere the gazetteer knows. Two things keep that number down. Headlines often name no place at all — "defence budget raised", "arms exports approved" — and those are genuinely unplaceable. And the gazetteer only knows the places written into it: it carries 217 entries, including capitals and demonyms, because headlines say Kyiv, Pentagon and Israeli far more often than they say the country. Anything it does not carry falls to unplaced. The list is in ADDITIONS and TERM_EXTRAS at the top of harvest_conflict.py and is meant to grow — adding a city, a force name or an armed group is one line, and the next harvest maps everything that names it.

Limits worth knowing. The gate is mechanical: it reads words, not meaning. Casualty figures in headlines are claims by whoever supplied them, and this feed neither verifies nor reconciles them. Coverage of any given war is uneven and partisan by language, and the language counts show the shape of that rather than correcting it. Google News caps a query at roughly 100 results over about 45 days.

Every wire read on the last harvest, with its standing: what it returned, and how many of its stories were refused or dropped as too local. A wire that could not be reached says so rather than disappearing.
