What this is. A monitor of armed conflict and the machinery around it: offensives and incursions, strikes and bombardment, attacks on civilians, the arms industry and its contracts, budgets and military aid, bases and deployments, officials and command, law and accountability, nuclear and strategic forces, humanitarian consequence, and cyber and information operations.

It does not take a side, and it does not summarise. Headlines and snippets are the publishers' own, truncated but never reworded; no claim is assessed, no party is described, and no model touches the text. Every row links to the original. On a subject where the same event is reported irreconcilably from opposite directions, the honest thing a feed can do is show who published what and let you read them against each other — which is what the source labels are for.

Direction. Escalation — offensives launched, strikes carried out, weapons delivered, budgets raised, forces deployed, talks abandoned. De-escalation — ceasefires, truces, withdrawals, prisoner exchanges, aid corridors opened, treaties signed, sanctions lifted. Feeds of this subject overwhelmingly show the first; the second is marked so it can be found. A story can carry both, and often does: a ceasefire announced after a night of strikes.

Who reports it. Bodies, courts & ministries is UN mechanisms, the ICRC, NATO, courts and defence ministries — parties as well as arbiters, and labelled so you know which. Research & monitors is SIPRI, ACLED, Human Rights Watch, Amnesty, Bellingcat, Airwars. Defence trade press covers the industry from inside it. Press is general news across 25 languages, including outlets on several sides of ongoing wars.

Evidence. Each story is scored on what it carries: a documented act — confirmed, announced, signed, carried out, a verdict, a warrant (2); institutional material — a UN body, a court, a ministry, SIPRI, ACLED, satellite imagery, a commission of inquiry (2); a measured figure (1); a scheduled or pending step (1); a named place (1); and a primary source (1). At three or more the row is marked well documented. A score is not a judgement of truth — an official statement scores well and can still be false. It records what kind of thing the story is.

The map. Two layers. Wide out you are looking at a painted plate — a drawn chart of the world, carried over from the projects map. Zoom past it and the plate cross-fades away between zoom 3 and zoom 5 to satellite imagery from Esri's free World Imagery layer: no account, no key, nothing tracked beyond the tiles your browser requests. The imagery carries the same treatment as the projects map — it supplies the light, a sea screen and a green soft-light wash shift the colour, a warm overlay does the sunlight, and an Esri hillshade multiplied on top puts the relief back. The tints ease off as you zoom in, where they would cover the detail you zoomed in for, and the relief strengthens, where it is wanted. No place labels: the ground is left to read as ground, and the pins carry the names. Every coastline on screen is still Esri's — nothing is redrawn, only lit differently. If the hillshade fails to load it is dropped and the plain imagery stands. Every story that resolved to a place gets its own pin. Stories resolving to the same place would sit on top of each other, so each is nudged onto a ring around the point: a country with forty stories shows forty pins fanned around it rather than one, and the placement is deterministic so a pin does not jump between renders. Clicking any pin opens a box listing every story within a few pixels of it — the one you clicked first, then its neighbours, each with its source and, where they differ, its own place. Zoomed out, that means Gaza and Rafah open together because they genuinely overlap; zoomed in they separate and each opens alone. Every title in the box links to its publisher exactly as a row does. The pins are built from the same filtered set as the list, so every toggle above applies to both. Stories that named nowhere in particular are counted as unplaced under the map rather than guessed at and dropped somewhere false.

How precise a pin is. The harvester looks for a named place first: a city, province, base, island or waterway — Kharkiv, Rafah, El Fasher, Goma, Ramstein, Al Udeid, Bab el-Mandeb, the Taiwan Strait. Roughly 260 of those are known, and a city beats the province containing it, so "M23 advances on Goma in North Kivu" pins Goma rather than the province. Only when no such place is named does the pin fall back to a country centroid, then a subregion, then a region. The row and the popup both name whichever level was used, so you can always see how precise a given pin is. A country-level pin is not the location of an event, and the small offset that keeps neighbouring pins apart is not a location either. A story about a strike in eastern Ukraine sits at the middle of Ukraine, because the feed reads headlines, not coordinates. Treat the map as a locator for what is being covered where, not as a map of events.

Region. Placement runs three levels deep: pick a region, then a subregion, then a place. Each row counts what it holds, and a story files under everything above whatever place it names. Choosing any of the three moves the map to frame that geography in full — Europe fits Europe, the Levant fits the Levant, Sweden centres on Sweden — and clearing returns it to the world. The frame comes from the gazetteer rather than from where the stories happen to be, so a region with two stories still shows you the whole region. Subject, direction, evidence and language filters leave the view alone, and so does panning: the map only moves when you ask it to move.

What is refused. The metaphors, which are relentless in this vocabulary: price wars, bidding wars, culture wars, wars of words, battles for market share, battleground states. Sport, where attacks and battles are constant. The shooter franchises. The status line reports how many stories each harvest refused.

Why some stories are not mapped. A story is placed only if its headline or snippet names somewhere the gazetteer or the place table knows. Two things keep that number down. Headlines often name no place at all — "defence budget raised", "arms exports approved" — and those are genuinely unplaceable. And the gazetteer only knows the places written into it: it carries 217 entries, including capitals and demonyms, because headlines say Kyiv, Pentagon and Israeli far more often than they say the country. Anything it does not carry falls to unplaced. The list is in ADDITIONS and TERM_EXTRAS at the top of harvest_conflict.py and is meant to grow — adding a city, a force name or an armed group is one line, and the next harvest maps everything that names it.

Limits worth knowing. The gate is mechanical: it reads words, not meaning. Casualty figures in headlines are claims by whoever supplied them, and this feed neither verifies nor reconciles them. Coverage of any given war is uneven and partisan by language, and the language counts show the shape of that rather than correcting it. Google News caps a query at roughly 100 results over about 45 days.

Every wire read on the last harvest, with its standing: what it returned, and how many of its stories were refused or dropped as too local. A wire that could not be reached says so rather than disappearing.
